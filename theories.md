# The theories I'm reasoning with

I did not invent the lenses in this repo. These are the established ideas I lean on to make sense of what is happening, each with a source. Naming them keeps me honest, because it shows where my reasoning is borrowed and testable rather than made up. This page is a living reference and it will grow.

## Identity versus authenticity
The distinction the whole repo turns on. Verifying that a real, specific person exists behind an account (identity) is a different problem from verifying that the content they post is real (authenticity). A liveness check answers the first. It says nothing about the second. C2PA is built entirely around this split, and states that a credential proves provenance integrity, not truth.[^c2pa] Almost every confusion in this space comes from treating the two as one.

## Verification as mutual transparency, not a checkpoint
The four columns of the matrix can be read two ways, and the reading changes everything. As platform-to-user assurance, verification is a verdict the company hands down: is a real human here, is this photo synthetic, is this account a duplicate. You receive the judgment, and the platform can rank you on it, which is how a safety tool slides toward the permission system the provenance-as-gatekeeping critique[^popularai] warns about. As user-to-user transparency, the same tools become a handshake. I voluntarily show you where my photos and words came from, you show me yours, and we each consent to meeting on accurate information. The direction of trust is the whole difference. Transparency that people opt into and offer each other is consent infrastructure. Transparency done to people and scored is surveillance. This reframing is what turns the informed-consent lens[^barkallah] into something buildable, because consent gets informed when both sides can see the provenance rather than one side being audited. It holds only while disclosure stays voluntary and symmetric. The moment a platform makes my disclosure mandatory, reads it, and ranks me on it, the handshake is a checkpoint again, and the implied-truth risk returns, because the silence of the people who do not disclose starts to look like a signal it is not.

## Profile as promise
Ellison, Hancock and Toma framed a dating profile as a promise that the real person will not deviate too far from it, a promise kept honest by the expectation of meeting in person.[^ellison2012] AI breaks the mechanism. Barkallah and Zytko find people fabricating whole personalities that the profile-as-promise model assumed the fear of a real date would prevent.[^barkallah]

## Truth-default theory
Levine's theory holds that people default to believing others and only enter suspicion mode when a cue trips it.[^levine] Ivan extends this to images: we treat photos as records of reality, so a synthetic photo that matches our expectations never triggers suspicion at all.[^ivan] This is why "just be more careful" is a weak defense. The whole point is that nothing tells you to be careful.

## The learning loop
Ivan's contribution. Users build detection heuristics (weird hands, plastic skin), model-makers fix those exact tells, and users have to start over, permanently one step behind.[^ivan] It reframes detection failure as a structural mismatch between how fast models improve and how slowly human habits update, not as a personal failing. It is the strongest argument that human detection cannot be the backstop.

## AI-mediated communication
Hancock's frame: communication that is not just transmitted by a machine but generated by one. Experiments in this line find that perceived AI involvement reduces trust in the sender,[^wukelly] which is why disclosure is fraught. Telling the truth about AI use can cost the discloser the very trust they were trying to earn.

## AI as partner versus AI as mediator
Boyd and Markowitz's MIRA model separates two roles AI plays in relationships: a direct relational partner you talk to, and an invisible mediator that reshapes the messages between two humans.[^boyd] On a dating app this is the whole map. An AI wingman or companion is a partner, and the tool quietly rewriting your opening line is a mediator, and the two raise different consent and trust questions. The framework grounds these in attachment, social exchange, and epistemic trust, and it makes the same point I keep landing on: the psychology is old, the medium is new.

## Informed consent and rape by deception
Barkallah and Zytko apply a consent lens: if agreement to meet or be intimate rests on content that misrepresents the person, the consent may be misinformed, and in the sexual context that connects to the legal idea of rape by deception.[^barkallah] This is the theory that turns "a bad date" into a safety and rights question, and it is why this is not only about hurt feelings.

## The implied truth effect
Pennycook and colleagues showed that labeling some false content makes people trust the unlabeled false content more, because the missing label reads as a stamp of approval.[^pennycook] Applied here, labeling only the AI you catch could quietly certify the AI you miss. It is the strongest theoretical argument against my own instinct to label.

## Provenance proves a chain, not the truth
The limit of the whole provenance approach. A signed credential can prove a file came from a named source and was not altered since. It cannot prove the source is honest or the content is real. A government can sign propaganda perfectly, and a truthful whistleblower photo may carry no credential at all.[^popularai] Provenance is a tool for good-faith signaling, not a truth detector, and forgetting that is how a safety idea can curdle into a gatekeeping one.
