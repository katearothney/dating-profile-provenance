# Why I started this

Start here. This is the reason the repo exists, what the problem actually is, what is missing, and what I think needs to happen.

## The night this started

I started this because of one profile on Hinge.

A man liked me. His profile carried the purple verified check, the badge that is supposed to mean he is who he says he is. Then I looked at the photos. A man in a black overcoat walking through falling snow. The same kind of man fishing by a lake, with the people in the background quietly warping the way AI images do. And then hockey photos, a Montreal Canadiens player, that did not even look like the same face as the other shots. The profile said he was authenticated. The photos were a jumble of AI-generated images and pictures that were not his, and the face barely held steady from one to the next.

That was the moment it clicked. The check had told me a real person was on the other end. It had told me nothing about whether anything on the profile was real. Those are two completely different promises, and the app was making the first one while quietly letting me assume the second.

## It is not one bad profile

I could have told myself Akrom was a fluke. Then I found someone teaching the method, and selling it. A public video titled "AI Dating Photos: 6 Mistakes That Get You Banned" coaches men on making AI photos that clear verification without getting reported, and points them to an AI-photo app and a paid community to buy in ([YouTube, accessed August 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OtPM34oOaRU)). His logic is the whole problem in two sentences. Use AI pics that still resemble your real face, he says, because "if you actually look like your AI pictures, you can get verified on dating apps like I did." And then, "if you're verified, what can the girl say, right?" The verified badge is not incidental to the deception. It is the centerpiece of the pitch, the thing that switches off the one bit of doubt that might have protected the person on the other side. This is not someone using AI to fix their lighting. It is deliberate, coached, monetized deception aimed at getting real dates from behind a fabricated profile, and women are named as the mark.

And it is not one teacher either. This is a small genre now. One creator runs a paid service and says he has made AI dating photos for more than 150 clients, split-testing the profiles across cities to rack up hundreds of likes each, with the whole method built on making the fake photos still resemble the real client so they hold up ([YouTube, accessed August 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4eme37OGihk)). Another films himself running AI photos on Tinder and Hinge for a week, shows the matches climb, and gets ahead of the obvious objection on camera. It "is not catfishing because it is you," he says, since the photos only drop his real face into places he never went ([YouTube, accessed August 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uF1L2Gbkt9I)). That is the consent problem said out loud by the person creating it. He cannot see the line between a flattering photo and a fabricated one, and the person on the receiving end is the one who has to find out on the date. All of it is being sold, and all of it treats a real date won on fabricated photos as the goal.

## Why it's a problem

The badge verifies identity. It does not verify content. A liveness check confirms a live human took a selfie that matches the uploaded photos, and both Hinge and Tinder say in their own help pages that it does not guarantee the safety of a user. So someone can pass verification with a face that is genuinely theirs and photos that are entirely fabricated. The verified badge, sitting next to AI-generated images, does not just fail to help. It actively misleads, because it trains people to trust exactly the thing they should question.

And this is not a harmless design gap. People cannot tell AI faces from real ones, with study after study putting detection at or below chance ([Ivan, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100208), [Nightingale and Farid, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2120481119), [Frank et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP54263.2024.00058)). Unlabeled synthetic content on a dating profile lands hardest on the people already most exposed, which is women, older adults, and marginalized users. AI-facilitated abuse is one of the fastest-growing forms of technology-facilitated violence against women. When the one signal an app gives me is misleading, the burden of catching a fabricated person falls entirely on me, on a first impression, which is exactly where humans are weakest.

## The gaps

Here is what is missing, and it is a short list.

Dating apps do not label AI-generated photos, video, or voice, even though Pinterest, Meta, and TikTok already label AI images at scale. The capability exists. The apps have not adopted it.

No law currently requires a dating app to label AI profile content. The transparency rules that do exist, the EU AI Act and California's SB 942, put the marking duty on the tools that generate the content, not on the platform that hosts it.

The tools that could catch it are unreliable on their own. Detection accuracy drops sharply from the lab to the real world, provenance credentials get stripped by a screenshot, and reverse image search does nothing against a face that never existed.

And nobody even knows how common this is. The one figure the industry offers on how many profiles use AI photos is an unaudited vendor number. We are missing the baseline.

## What needs to happen

Apps should say when something on a profile was made or altered by AI, the same way Pinterest does. That is the whole ask, and it is scoped on purpose to the content you judge someone on first: images, video, and voice or audio. Text is the harder problem and comes later.
